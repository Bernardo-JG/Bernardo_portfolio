Photovoltaic module efficiency

Comparing machine-learning and neural-network models to predict and classify solar-panel efficiency.

The problem

How well can a model estimate photovoltaic-module efficiency from operating conditions and module characteristics? Our team explored two formulations: predicting a continuous efficiency value and classifying efficiency as extremely bad, bad, moderate or good.

The project compares traditional machine learning with dense neural networks on tabular data. It provided an opportunity to work through preprocessing, model selection, feature importance and the trade-off between model complexity and performance.

My contribution

I was primarily responsible for model training and evaluation, including the deep-learning comparison. I compared the traditional models, examined their predictions and metrics, and explored whether dense neural networks improved on those baselines.

Data & approach

Inputs include temperature, irradiance, affected area, voltage, current, module type and condition categories such as soiling and hotspots. Categorical inputs are one-hot encoded and numerical inputs are standardised using the training partition. Saved experiments contain 80,000 training rows and 20,000 test rows, with 17 encoded inputs.

We compared k-nearest neighbours, linear and logistic regression, a custom Naive Bayes combination, random forest and XGBoost. Five-fold cross-validation on the training partition supported model comparison, followed by evaluation on the 20% test partition and experiments with reduced feature sets.

For deep learning, separate regression and classification MLPs use two 64-unit hidden layers, dropout and early stopping. This allowed us to ask whether extra model complexity improved on the traditional baselines.

Recorded results

The saved baseline results show strong performance on this dataset. These are the repository’s recorded experiments, not an independent rerun.

A later reduced-feature XGBoost experiment records 99.135% classification accuracy. The distinction matters: this is a separate variant from the full-feature baseline. The dense networks did not outperform the strongest traditional models, while linear regression remained a useful simple baseline for predicting efficiency.

Lessons & limits

The most useful result was the model comparison itself: more complexity did not automatically produce better predictions. Feature removal also had different effects on regression and classification, so its value depended on the task.

The scores describe a random split of the supplied repository dataset. They do not establish performance on independent solar installations. The notebooks also leave room for stronger evaluation: fitting preprocessing separately within each cross-validation fold and reserving a fresh holdout after comparing feature-selection variants.
